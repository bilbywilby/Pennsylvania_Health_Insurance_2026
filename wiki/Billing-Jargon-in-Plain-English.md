# Billing Jargon in Plain English

Billing departments speak in abbreviations. Here's the translation -- each term gets one plain sentence plus a concrete example.

| Term | Plain English | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **EOB** (Explanation of Benefits) | Your insurer's accounting of what it paid and what it expects others to owe -- it is **not a bill**. | An EOB saying "patient owes $400" is the insurer's estimate; the actual bill comes separately and may disagree. Pay the *bill*, question the *EOB* if they conflict. |
| **Balance billing** | A provider billing you for the gap between its charge and what your insurer allowed -- legal in some situations, illegal in many (see surprise-billing rules). | You saw an in-network hospital but the ER doctor was out-of-network and sends you a $1,900 bill for the gap -- likely a protected surprise bill. |
| **Itemized bill** | The per-line breakdown of every charge (date, code, quantity, price). You can demand one -- say so in writing. | A single line saying "surgical services -- $8,400" tells you nothing; an itemized bill may reveal three charges for the same supply. |
| **Upcoding** | Billing a more expensive service code than the care actually delivered. | A routine 15-minute office visit billed as an extended complex consultation. |
| **Unbundling** | Billing a bundled procedure as separate pieces to collect more. | Charging separately for the surgical tray, closure, and dressing that should be one bundled price. |
| **Duplicate charge** | The same service billed twice. | Two identical medication-administration charges on one day with no second dose documented. |
| **CPT / HCPCS codes** | Standard 5-digit (or letter-number) codes identifying each service for billing. | CPT 99214 = an established-patient office visit of moderate complexity -- useful when checking whether a code matches your visit length. |
| **MPFS** (Medicare Physician Fee Schedule) | Medicare's published price list for physician services -- a widely used reasonableness benchmark, not a price you automatically owe. | If a provider charged $3,200 for a procedure Medicare pays $340 for, that gap is a negotiation talking point, not a verdict. |
| **NCCI edits** | Medicare's rulebook of code pairs that shouldn't be billed together -- helps identify improper combos. | Certain lab panels billed both as a bundle and as components on the same date is a classic NCCI-flagged combination. |
| **FMV** (Fair Market Value) | What the service plausibly costs in the local market -- used to judge whether a charge is inflated. | Two regional hospital systems charging $700 and $2,600 for the same imaging study suggests the second is out of line. |
| **Fully insured vs. self-funded (ERISA)** | Who regulates your appeal: a state-regulated insurance product, or an employer's own fund governed by federal law. | Ask your HR department: "Is our health plan fully insured or self-funded?" The answer decides your review route (state PID vs. federal ERISA). |
| **AGB** (Amounts Generally Billed) | The ceiling a nonprofit hospital may charge FAP-eligible patients -- roughly what Medicare or insurers typically pay. | If Medicare would have paid $1,100 and a charity-eligible patient is billed $4,900, that excess may violate the hospital's own obligations. |
| **Allowed amount** | The maximum your plan will recognize for a service; excess is discounted in-network, billable only within legal limits out-of-network. | Charge $500, allowed $220, plan pays 80% of allowed: in-network you'd owe $44, not $84. |

## Red Flags to Spot on an Itemized Bill

You don't need formulas -- look for these patterns:

1. **Anything dated on a day you weren't there.**
2. **Two charges with the same description and date** (duplicates).
3. **Charges for supplies "included" in a procedure** billed separately anyway (unbundling).
4. **Codes describing more care than happened** -- a 5-minute visit coded as an hour-long exam (upcoding).
5. **Out-of-network ancillary people** on an in-network visit -- pathologist, anesthesiologist, radiologist (frequently protected by surprise-billing rules -- check).
6. **Charges wildly above Medicare's published rate** for the same code -- more than ~4-5x is worth challenging.
7. **Numbers that don't reconcile:** itemized total != the statement total, or statement != what the EOB says you owe.

Found red flags? Start with the [[Surprise Bill Playbook|Surprise-Bill-Playbook]] or [[Medical Debt Defense Playbook|Medical-Debt-Defense-Playbook]] depending on who sent the bill.

## What This Means in Real Life (Step-by-Step Example)

Suppose a hospital bills you **$1,800** for an urgent clinic visit and lab work, and your insurer's Explanation of Benefits (**EOB**) says **"Patient Responsibility: $1,150"** because the lab was billed separately.

1. **Do not pay immediately just because a bill looks official.** An EOB shows what the insurer processed, not always a final legally owed balance after coding or network checks.
2. **Ask the billing office for an itemized statement** showing each service code (`CPT` or `HCPCS`), unit count, and billed dollar amount, and compare every line against your EOB.
3. **Check whether the bill involves an emergency, an out-of-network specialist at an in-network hospital, or a hospital financial assistance program (`FAP`)** before agreeing to a payment plan or using a credit card (which can turn protected medical debt into standard credit card debt).
